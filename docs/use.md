# Use

How the SpaceCup prototypes were used during bench and flight testing. This describes the tested procedure, not a fitting or medical instruction: see the disclaimer at the end.

## Insertion

Fold the cup, for example into a C shape, to narrow it for insertion, then release it once past the vaginal opening so it can spring back open.

## Activation

For the Flower mechanisms, activation means pulling the strings that run from the petals to the pull mechanism in the stem, closing the petals over the opening to seal the cup (thesis p.10). The pull should be firm enough to close the mechanism, but not so hard that it extracts the cup along with the strings: an early flight attempt did exactly that, drawing the whole cup out on the first pull before later attempts closed it in place without extracting it (`data/flight-questionnaire.csv`).

## Extraction

Extraction follows activation: once the mechanism is closed and the fluid sealed in, the cup is drawn out by the stem. The bench evaluation, run under lab conditions, scored insertion, extraction and resetting together as usability, joint highest for Flower Low (with the Duckbill) (thesis pp.20-21); in reduced gravity, analogue-astronaut testers reported that both insertion and extraction were generally easy to perform (thesis p.22; see [`validation.md`](validation.md)).

## Resetting the Flower

To reuse a Flower cup, the tail (the pull mechanism in the stem) has to be pushed back to its open, unactivated position after each activation. One flight participant reported this reset as requiring some force (`data/flight-questionnaire.csv`).

## Emptying by syringe

The usability protocol inserted each prototype into the artificial vagina, filled it with artificial menstrual fluid, extracted it, removed the fluid with a syringe, cleaned the cup, and reset the closing mechanism for the next cycle (thesis p.15).

## Cleaning

Three cleaning methods were tested and compared against a reference cup, with colony counts in [`../data/cleaning-colony-counts.csv`](../data/cleaning-colony-counts.csv):

- **70 % isopropanol (IPA)**, wiped then immersed and shaken: the lowest, most consistent colony counts of the three methods, the best disinfection performance tested. Shaking in a confined container likely helped the disinfectant reach narrow gaps and cavities (thesis p.23, p.26).
- **Microwave**, wiped then 30 s upside-down in 20 mL of water in a microwave: performed well for the simpler-geometry Drawstring and Twister designs, but gave noticeably higher colony counts on the Flower designs.
- **Boiling**, 5 min, wiped but not rinsed beforehand: the reference method, but with variable results, likely because residue trapped in cavities was not rinsed away before boiling (thesis p.23).

Geometry matters as much as method: the Flower designs' closely spaced strings and narrow gaps held residue that was harder to reach with steam or boiling water, even though the same gaps were reachable when the cup was shaken in isopropanol (thesis p.23, p.26).

The cleaning assessment was carried out under non-sterile laboratory conditions: cups were contaminated with a standardised load of live-culture yoghurt and then handled with clean, but non-sterile, hands before cleaning and culture on agar plates (thesis pp.18-24). Results are therefore a comparison between designs and procedures, not a validated clinical disinfection protocol.

## Statement

The SpaceCup is a research prototype, tested on the bench and in short parabolic-flight windows. It is not a certified medical device, and it is not for personal use.
